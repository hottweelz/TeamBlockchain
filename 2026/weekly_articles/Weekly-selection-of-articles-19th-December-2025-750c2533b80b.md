[**More US Stablecoins on the way as OCC gives conditional approval for 5 Trust Bank Charters**](https://www.occ.gov/news-issuances/news-releases/2025/nr-occ-2025-125.html)The US banking perimeter has shifted decisively with the Office of the Comptroller of the Currency granting conditional national trust bank charters to Circle, Ripple and Paxos, alongside conversions for BitGo and Fidelity Digital Assets. The move brings leading stablecoin and custody providers under federal supervision, signalling that digital asset infrastructure is no longer an adjacent experiment but part of the regulated core of finance. For banks, the implications are strategic rather than cosmetic. National trust status allows these firms to offer custody, settlement and payments services with the credibility of OCC oversight, putting them in direct competition with banks’ own trust, custody and payments franchises. Stablecoins such as USDC gain institutional legitimacy, accelerating their use in treasury management, cross-border payments and tokenised markets. Yet this is not deregulation by stealth. Trust charters limit lending and deposit-taking, preserving banks’ balance-sheet advantages. But they do erode banks’ historic monopoly over money movement and safekeeping. Commercial banks now face a choice: partner with regulated digital-native trusts, build competing tokenised deposit and custody platforms, or risk disintermediation in high-growth areas such as settlement, collateral management and digital asset custody. The OCC’s decision underscores a broader truth: competition in banking is no longer coming only from FinTechs skirting regulation, but from fully supervised digital institutions reshaping the infrastructure layer itself.

[**$124trillion to be inherited in the next 22 years and the new owners are younger, mobile and digitally engaged.**](https://fortune.com/2025/12/06/great-wealth-transfer-inheritance-gen-z-millennials-boomers-entrepreneurs-self-made-billionaires/)The acceleration of the so-called Great Wealth Transfer is reshaping global capital flows in ways that asset managers can no longer treat as incremental. UBS estimates that heirs inherited a record $297.8bn in 2025 alone, while Cerulli projects $124tn will change hands globally by 2048. This is not merely a demographic milestone but a structural shift in how capital is owned, deployed and stewarded. The profile of new wealth holders is changing rapidly. Younger beneficiaries, often in their teens or twenties, are inheriting complex, multi-jurisdictional portfolios at a time of heightened market volatility and geopolitical uncertainty. Alongside this, a steady pipeline of newly minted, self-made billionaires is adding entrepreneurial risk appetites to the mix. The result is a client base that is simultaneously younger, more globally mobile and more demanding of transparency, purpose and digital access. For asset managers, the implications are profound. Traditional, relationship-led models built around capital preservation will need to coexist with strategies that emphasise private markets, thematic investing and impact. Intergenerational planning, governance structures and family education are becoming core products, not ancillary services. Firms that can combine institutional-grade risk management with flexible, digitally enabled advice stand to capture long-duration assets. Those that cannot, may find that the largest wealth transfer in history is also the most disruptive to incumbents.

According to Kyriba’s 2025 CFO survey, AI is no longer viewed primarily as a back-office efficiency play but as a core instrument for managing liquidity, risk and volatility in a far more fragile macro environment. Treasurers are deploying AI to forecast cash flows with greater precision, optimise working capital in real time and anticipate FX, interest-rate and counterparty shocks before they crystallise. The emphasis has shifted from automation to foresight. What stands out is the strategic intent. CFOs increasingly see AI-driven treasury platforms as critical infrastructure rather than optional upgrades, particularly as geopolitics, fragmented payments systems and higher rates strain traditional forecasting models. The survey suggests a growing expectation that finance functions should operate continuously, not periodically, with AI acting as an always-on risk radar rather than a quarterly reporting aid. **Implications for banks.** For banks, this evolution is double-edged. On one hand, it creates demand for more sophisticated liquidity, FX and risk-management services, tighter API integration and real-time data feeds. On the other, AI-enabled corporates are becoming more self-directed, less tolerant of opacity and quicker to arbitrage pricing and service gaps. Banks that remain product-centric risk disintermediation; those that embed themselves as intelligent balance-sheet partners may find their role deepening. In treasury, as elsewhere, AI is quietly redrawing the boundary between client and intermediary.

[**Coinbase looks to further threaten traditional stock exchanges**](https://www.cnbc.com/2025/12/17/coinbase-prediction-markets-stock-trading-stablecoins.html)Coinbase’s latest expansion marks a decisive attempt to blur the boundary between crypto exchanges and traditional stock markets. By adding equities, derivatives, prediction markets and a clear roadmap toward tokenised stocks, Coinbase is positioning itself less as a specialist venue and more as an always-on financial utility. For traditional exchanges, the challenge is structural rather than competitive on price: Coinbase offers continuous engagement, integrated wallets, and on-chain settlement rails that operate far beyond market hours. The strategic threat lies in distribution and market design. Prediction markets and perpetual trading turn finance into a real-time, sentiment-driven product, while tokenisation promises faster settlement, fractional access and global reach—features legacy exchanges still struggle to deliver within T+1 frameworks. If tokenised equities gain regulatory traction, Coinbase could intermediate trading, custody and settlement in a single stack, compressing roles long separated across exchanges, clearing houses and custodians. For incumbents such as the NYSE or LSE, this is less about crypto and more about relevance. Either they adopt tokenised rails, extended trading models and API-first access, or risk being confined to a shrinking slice of daytime cash equity trading. Coinbase’s move signals that the future exchange may look more like a platform than a marketplace, and may not ring a closing bell at all.

[**Blockchain technology, a tool to help combat KYCLAML challenges**](https://www.ainvest.com/news/rise-blockchain-intelligence-strategic-tool-fight-crypto-crime-investment-opportunities-transparency-revolution-2512/)The maturation of digital assets is quietly redrawing the compliance landscape. What was once framed as a regulatory burden is fast becoming a source of competitive advantage, as blockchain-powered intelligence moves from optional tooling to core financial infrastructure. With stablecoin regimes crystallising and spot bitcoin ETFs normalising institutional exposure, regulators now expect crypto firms to meet, and in some cases exceed, traditional AML and KYC standards. This has propelled explosive growth in blockchain analytics. Firms such as Chainalysis and TRM Labs sit at the centre of a market forecast to quintuple by 2032, driven by the complexity of modern financial crime: cross-chain laundering, privacy-enhanced tokens and decentralised finance architectures that dissolve conventional reporting lines. Their tools transform public blockchains into auditable systems, offering real-time transaction monitoring, attribution and forensic trails that banks have long lacked. For AML and KYC, the implications are profound. Compliance is shifting from static identity checks to continuous behavioural surveillance. “Know your customer” is being supplemented by “know your transaction” and, increasingly, “know your network”. Regulators gain deeper visibility, enforcement becomes faster, and institutions are better equipped to evidence compliance ex post. The result is a paradox: crypto’s radical transparency is making it one of the most surveyed financial systems ever built. Those who master blockchain intelligence will not merely survive regulation – they will define the standards by which digital finance is trusted.
7. **HMT issues updates to proposed digital asset regulation** The 2025 FSMA (Cryptoassets) Regulations pull most UK crypto‑asset activity into the mainstream regulatory perimeter, which strengthens the UK’s institutional appeal but may push some lighter‑regulated business offshore. The main **impact on the UK financial sector** will be Public offers, exchange admissions, trading, custody, staking and stablecoin issuance of “qualifying cryptoassets” become regulated or designated activities, with prospectus‑style disclosure, market‑abuse rules, insider lists and FCA enforcement powers. This gives banks, brokers, trading venues and custodians a clear rulebook to build compliant products, integrate crypto and tokenised assets into existing market infrastructure, and offer them to professional clients.​New liability and disclosure standards around “qualifying stablecoins” and token admissions push serious issuers and platforms toward higher governance and risk management, favouring larger firms with compliance budgets and creating opportunities for regulated exchanges, custodians and advisory practices.​

**UK’s global standing in digital assets**. By creating a MiCA‑like regime that covers both market‑abuse and primary‑market disclosure for cryptoassets, the UK positions itself as one of the few G7 jurisdictions with a comprehensive, securities‑style framework for public token offerings and trading, which is attractive for institutional investors looking for regulatory certainty.​ However, the breadth of the regime, the 2027 full‑commencement date, and the complexity of permissions, disclosure rules and transitional provisions mean **higher fixed costs for smaller issuers and DeFi‑style** projects, so some innovation may continue to gravitate toward more permissive hubs while the UK competes primarily as a regulated, institutional‑grade centre for digital and tokenised assets rather than a retail‑crypto hotspot.
