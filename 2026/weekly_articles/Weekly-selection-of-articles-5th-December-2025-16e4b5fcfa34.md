[**Stablecoins could lead to better payments, but risks remain.**](https://www.riksbank.se/globalassets/media/rapporter/staff-memo/engelska/2025/stablecoins-could-lead-to-better-payments-but-risks-remain2.pdf)Stablecoins have quietly become the sharpest wedge between today’s bank-based money system and tomorrow’s tokenised finance, the Riksbank staff memo argues, and their trajectory now poses as many geopolitical and systemic questions as it does efficiency gains. Born as plumbing for crypto trading and DeFi, dollar-referenced coins have exploded from about USD 4 billion in 2020 to roughly USD 272 billion by October 2025, with two issuers, Tether and Circle, controlling the lion’s share and 99% of value pegged to the US dollar. That scale turns “private money” into a macro variable: stablecoins can cheapen cross-border payments and offer a lifeline in high-inflation jurisdictions, but they also deepen de facto dollarisation, export US policy and concentrate power in a handful of foreign tech-finance platforms.​Regulators are responding, but unevenly. The EU’s MiCA regime forces full backing, par redemption and tight supervision, while the US GENIUS Act takes a looser, pro-growth stance designed explicitly to boost dollar use and Treasury demand. The memo’s deeper warning is about monetary “singleness” and financial stability. If stablecoins trade at haircuts, or face fire-sale dynamics when reserves are dumped into stressed markets, the seamless par relationship between different forms of money begins to fray, dragging banks, money markets and central banks into unfamiliar territory. The Riksbank authors stop short of prescribing bans; instead, they hint at a future in which central banks, domestic-currency stablecoins and tokenised deposits compete for relevance. In that world, the real contest is not crypto versus fiat, but who controls the settlement layer of digital money, and on whose terms.

The surge in energy-hungry data centres required to train and run advanced AI models has sharpened concerns about grid stress, higher consumer prices and a potential slowdown in climate targets. Yet the same technologies threatening to inflate demand are also beginning to optimise supply. Across buildings, transport and heavy industry, AI systems are already cutting energy use through predictive analytics and real-time optimisation. On power grids, machine-learning tools now forecast renewable output, balance fluctuating supply and demand, and identify failing equipment before outages occur. The larger prize lies ahead: accelerating infrastructure planning and materials discovery. AI-driven modelling is helping utilities map long-term investment needs, evaluate extreme-weather scenarios and compress planning cycles that previously took years. In laboratories, AI-guided experimentation is speeding the discovery of advanced materials for batteries, reactors and electrolyzers, potentially cutting development timelines from decades to years. The emerging risk is governance. AI may enable a cleaner grid, but only if regulators, engineers and policymakers move in concert. Without coordination, rising data-centre loads may outpace the efficiency gains AI delivers. SO, WHAT. Well lower energy prices is deflationary and so help to lower interest rates globally.

[**What are the potential implications of Klarna’s stablecoin for privately issued stablecoins and global payment infrastructure?**](https://hernaes.com/)Klarna’s decision to launch a US Dollar stablecoin marks a notable shift in how major FinTechs envision the future of payments. Unlike earlier corporate experiments, KlarnaUSD is positioned not as a crypto asset but as **core settlement infrastructure**, leveraging Stripe’s Bridge issuance platform and the Tempo blockchain to cut cross-border payment costs. The move signals that privately issued, fully reserved stablecoins are entering a new competitive phase, one shaped by scale, compliance capacity and merchant distribution rather than decentralised ideology The implications extend well beyond Klarna. A payment-native stablecoin issued by a consumer-facing fintech accelerates consolidation in a market already tilting toward institutional issuers. Smaller or algorithmic stablecoins risk being pushed to the margins, confined to DeFi or fading altogether. For incumbents, the challenge is structural. Stablecoin settlement bypasses correspondent banking and erodes high-margin card-scheme economics in international payments. While cards retain trust and distribution, a multi-rail model, where stablecoins become an optional settlement layer, appears increasingly likely. Domestic schemes face a subtler threat: tokenised settlement risks relegating local payment rails to low-value funding pipes while data and economics move on-chain. Regulators, wary of “private dollarisation,” may tighten rules or fast-track public alternatives. KlarnaUSD is therefore less a disruption than a **signal**: the competitive frontier in payments is shifting from the checkout counter to the settlement layer beneath it.

[**AI-Driven Payments Operating Models Are Here**](https://www.ukfinance.org.uk/news-and-insight/blog/ai-driven-payments-operating-models-are-here) **-** Artificial intelligence is no longer an add-on to payments infrastructure; it is becoming the organising principle beneath it. As generative and traditional AI systems increasingly influence routing, risk scoring, authentication and liquidity decisions, banks face a strategic crossroads: retrofit outdated architectures, or rebuild their operating models for real-time, event-driven finance. AI has already reshaped fraud detection, enabling continuous monitoring and dynamic interdiction, even as losses reached £33billon globally in 2024. Biometric authentication, voice-activated payments and automated exception handling are moving from innovation labs into production. The most profound impact, however, lies behind the scenes. Payments remain dominated by legacy batch systems with low straight-through processing rates and opaque status messages. To unlock AI’s potential, institutions require deep rewiring: real-time observability, predictive liquidity engines, compliant data pipelines and ISO 20022-rich messages broadcast instantly across the enterprise. Regulators are tightening expectations. The ECB, PRA and DORA demand traceable model governance, forcing banks to establish cross-functional AI councils, kill-switch oversight mechanisms and automated CI/CD-driven model inventories. Sector leaders such as JPMorgan, HSBC, Revolut are already treating AI as critical infrastructure, not experimentation. Their example signals the emerging competitive divide. Institutions that frame AI as a software upgrade will lag; those that rebuild around AI-native operating models will shape the next decade of payments innovation. [**In the UK the Property (Digital Assets etc) Act 2025 comes into force**](https://www.assetservicingtimes.com/assetservicesnews/digitalassetsarticle.php?article_id=17438)**-** The UK’s decision to create a third legal category of property for digital assets marks one of the most consequential reforms in modern commercial law. By granting cryptocurrencies, stablecoins and NFTs full personal property rights, Parliament has resolved years of judicial ambiguity and positioned English law as a global standard-setter for digital asset governance. Bearing in mind as [Lord Holmes](https://chrisholmes.co.uk/news/electronic-trade-documents-act/) reminded us: “*English law has a considerable impact on global trade, 80% of bills of lading and 60% of global trade finance operate on English law.”* The change is subtle in drafting but radical in effect. Digital assets can now be unequivocally owned, inherited, collateralised, and recovered in theft or insolvency, placing them on the same legal footing as conventional financial instruments. The move is not merely protective; it is strategic. London’s legal system has long been a magnet for international commercial disputes. A clear statutory foundation for digital property strengthens its appeal at a moment when $trillions in tokenised assets are expected to enter global markets. The reform also sets the stage for sophisticated lending, custody and asset-management products, as banks and FinTechs gain legal certainty over enforceability. But the deeper implication is structural, once digital assets are recognised as property, they become building blocks for a tokenised economy. Smart-contract-secured collateral, programmable settlements, and on-chain securities regulation all become legally actionable. The Bill is therefore less an endpoint than a gateway, one that accelerates the UK toward becoming a pre-eminent jurisdiction for tokenised finance and digital-asset capital formation.

[**Banks pushing digital payments for cross boarder transaction**](https://www.pymnts.com/cryptocurrency/2025/complexity-of-blockchain-rails-trip-up-stablecoins-enterprise-push/)**.** Digital payments are increasingly being used by banks as an alternative to the existing cross-border payment infrastructure rails. Their appeal is obvious: near-instant settlement, predictable costs and the ability to bypass the slow, multi-layered correspondent banking model that underpins today’s international transfers. Recent announcements include **Sony Bank** preparing has announced it is to issue US Dollar stablecoin, Europe’s largest banks designing a Euro-pegged token, Ripple enabling new stablecoin corridors, signalling a transition from experimentation to industrial adoption. Yet behind each transaction lies a highly fragmented technical maze: public blockchains, Layer-2 networks, custodians, tokenisation providers and settlement engines. Blockchain-powered digital payments have not eliminated clearing; it has disassembled it. Where traditional payments centralise reconciliation within a handful of institutions, stablecoin flows distribute that function across validators, sequencers, exchanges and bridges. **For banks, the implications are structural.** Stablecoins threaten to erode fee-rich FX and correspondent revenue, shifting value capture to issuers and network operators. But they also create new opportunities: bank-issued stablecoins, tokenised deposits and institutional clearing layers that restore trust and compliance to decentralised rails. The competitive frontier is moving from the card terminal to the settlement layer. Banks that treat stablecoins as a peripheral curiosity risk losing cross-border relevance; those that build or integrate orchestration infrastructure may define the next generation of global payments.
7. **B**[**uilding the tech backbone for digital asset management**](https://www.mckinsey.com/capabilities/tech-and-ai/our-insights/tech-forward/building-the-tech-backbone-for-digital-asset-management)A McKinsey report looking at the asset management sector believes asset managers, long insulated by rising markets and predictable fee structures, are now confronting a structural shift: tokenisation is moving from experiment to expectation. As investors demand real-time reporting, fractional access to private markets and lower fees, the traditional operating model, built on fragmented data, batch processing and legacy systems, can no longer compete with tokenised fund structures offering instantaneous settlement, embedded compliance and automated NAV calculation. The strategic implication is clear: tokenisation is not simply a new wrapper for old products but a catalyst for full-stack digitalisation. To issue or manage tokenised securities, firms need unified data estates, event-driven architectures and interoperable ledger systems, capabilities many asset managers currently lack. Those that modernise can reduce reconciliation costs, enhance risk oversight and open new distribution channels via programmable smart-contract rails. Those that don’t risk being disintermediated by custodians, exchanges and FinTechs building token-native infrastructure. Tokenisation also accelerates margin pressure. As funds become more automated, fee compression will intensify, and scale advantages will grow. Asset managers must therefore reorient treat technology as core infrastructure, not an efficiency project; focus on mastering data governance; and build token-ready operating models that integrate with emerging digital market infrastructures. Furthermore, as funds and securities tokenise they will demand that not only, they are bought and sold using digital money, but distributions will become more frequent, transitioning from six monthly payments to monthly or weekly. This is likely to mean asset managers will need to make such distributions more automated/ programmable and use digital payment alternatives. The firms that succeed will not be those that simply digitise funds, but those that rebuild asset management for a programmable, always-on market.
