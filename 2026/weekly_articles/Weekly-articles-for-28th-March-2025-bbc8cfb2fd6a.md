[America must harness stablecoins to future-proof the dollar](https://finance.yahoo.com/news/america-must-harness-stablecoins-future-160141091.html)- Guiding and Establishing National Innovation for U.S. Stablecoins Act of 2025 (GENIUS Act) could allow a regulated fintech or trust company to issue a dollar stablecoin under state supervision. The bank Citi group estimates up to [$5 trillion](%28https%3A/www.marketsmedia.com/house-committee-holds-hearing-on-stablecoins/%29) in assets could move into stablecoins and digital money by 2030, up from roughly $200 billion now​. “[*If the U.S. fails to act, it risks “becoming the rust belt of the financial industry,” as one fintech CEO warned*](https://finance.yahoo.com/news/america-must-harness-stablecoins-future-160141091.html)” Clearly the more money tied up in US$ stablecoins the greater to spread of influence of the dollar and the more demand there will be for US Treasuries to help support US deficits and potentially help to keep interest rates lower

[Digital Euro plans continue **-** Public Chains, Private Bias: Can Capital Markets Embrace True Blockchain Innovation?](https://www.omfif.org/2025/03/eurosystem-cash-solution-for-dlt-transactions-a-start-but-not-enough/?mc_cid=4dc276cc92&mc_eid=8fd65fb32f) Momentum is building for blockchain adoption in capital markets. The U.S. rollback of SAB 121 and the Eurosystem’s push for DLT-compatible cash settlement mark meaningful progress. Yet, just as the road clears, an outdated regulatory detour threatens to derail the journey. The stumbling block? The Basel Committee’s prudential treatment of digital assets. Despite industry advances and public blockchain maturity, banks face punitive capital requirements for holding high-quality tokenized assets—simply because they’re issued on public, permissionless networks like Ethereum. Even AAA-rated bonds, if issued on public chains, are lumped in with volatile altcoins under “Type 2b,” subject to 1:1 capital reserves and strict exposure caps. This classification risks sidelining open networks that are arguably more transparent, resilient, and interoperable than their private counterparts. It also undermines market function: banks won’t hold or trade these instruments, impacting liquidity and innovation. And yet, the European Banking Authority shows there is another path—recognising tokenised traditional assets for what they are, regardless of blockchain type. The real question isn’t whether crypto is too risky—it’s whether regulators can differentiate between noise and nuance. Can we build a regulatory framework that acknowledges both the promise and reality of public blockchains? Until we do, we risk treating infrastructure built for the future with rules written for the past.

[Are we set to see the world’s biggest asset class truly embrace programmable payments?](https://www.ledgerinsights.com/nyse-owner-ice-to-explore-usdc-stablecoin-tokenized-fund-for-capital-markets/)- ​The Intercontinental Exchange (ICE), owner of the New York Stock Exchange (NYSE), has announced plans to explore the integration of Circle's USDC stablecoin and the recently acquired USYC money market fund into its operations. This initiative aims to develop new products and solutions across derivatives exchanges, clearinghouses, and data services, potentially establishing new markets based on these digital assets. Lynn Martin, President of NYSE, emphasised the growing trust in digital currencies among market participants, stating, "*We believe Circle's stablecoins and tokenized digital currencies can play a larger role in capital markets as digital currencies become more trusted by market participants as an acceptable equivalent to the US Dollar."* ​ Additionally, the CME Group has begun experimenting with tokenization, and the Commodity Futures Trading Commission (CFTC) has announced pilots for tokenised collateral involving tokenised assets and stablecoins. ​Circle's USDC stablecoin, with an issuance exceeding $60 billion, has been primarily utilised within the cryptocurrency sector. Unlike offshore counterparts like Tether, Circle has pursued an onshore, regulated approach, aiming to integrate USDC into mainstream financial markets. However, challenges remain, particularly concerning the preference for central bank money in capital market settlements and the legal uncertainties surrounding stablecoins in bankruptcy scenarios. ​ As ICE and other financial giants explore the potential of stablecoins and tokenized funds, the landscape of capital markets may experience significant transformation, bridging the gap between traditional finance and the burgeoning digital asset ecosystem.

[Stablecoin Tipping Point of Cross-Border Payments: What Banks Must Do Next](https://paymentscmi.com/insights/stablecoins-cross-border-payments-banks-strategy/)- Stablecoins are rapidly reshaping the global payments landscape—delivering faster, cheaper, and more transparent transactions than traditional systems. With over $6.3 trillion in stablecoin payments settled in the last year (15% of global retail cross-border volume), adoption is gaining traction across both Web3 startups and traditional giants like Visa, JP Morgan, and PayPal.

But regulation has been the missing piece—until now. In 2025, a wave of regulatory clarity is arriving from the EU (MiCA), UK, US (STABLE & GENIUS Acts), and Asia, unlocking stablecoins for broader institutional use. As legal uncertainty fades, banks can finally tap into stablecoin benefits: freeing up capital tied in Nostro accounts, cutting remittance costs by up to 80%, and speeding settlements to minutes. This isn’t just about payments—it’s a competitive inflection point. Banks that embrace stablecoins now stand to unlock billions in liquidity, enter underserved markets, and future-proof their offerings. But hesitation will mean losing ground to nimble FinTechs and blockchain-native players.

Stablecoins aren’t hype. They’re infrastructure. And the clock is ticking.
