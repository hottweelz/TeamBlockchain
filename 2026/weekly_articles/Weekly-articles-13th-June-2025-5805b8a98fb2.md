1. The stablecoin issuance and cryptoasset custody consultation paper by [**Financial Conduct Authority**](https://www.linkedin.com/company/financial-conduct-authority/). The proposals aim to create clarity, reduce risk, and support responsible innovation in digital finance.

   1. 𝐒𝐭𝐚𝐛𝐥𝐞𝐜𝐨𝐢𝐧𝐬 𝐦𝐮𝐬𝐭 𝐟𝐮𝐧𝐜𝐭𝐢𝐨𝐧 𝐚𝐬 𝐭𝐫𝐮𝐬𝐭𝐞𝐝, 𝐦𝐨𝐧𝐞𝐲-𝐥𝐢𝐤𝐞 𝐢𝐧𝐬𝐭𝐫𝐮𝐦𝐞𝐧𝐭𝐬
   •Issuers will need to fully back fiat-referenced stablecoins with secure, liquid assets such as short-term government debt and cash deposits, held in a statutory trust with an independent third-party custodian.
   •Redemption at par value must be available to all holders, including retail users, and processed by the next business day.
   •This directly addresses the risks seen in past de-pegs, where retail users lacked fair redemption access.

   2. 𝐂𝐮𝐬𝐭𝐨𝐝𝐲 𝐬𝐭𝐚𝐧𝐝𝐚𝐫𝐝𝐬 𝐫𝐞𝐬𝐩𝐨𝐧𝐝 𝐭𝐨 𝐫𝐞𝐚𝐥-𝐰𝐨𝐫𝐥𝐝 𝐟𝐚𝐢𝐥𝐮𝐫𝐞𝐬
   •Custodians must segregate client assets, maintain accurate records, and hold them in trust.
   •In insolvency scenarios, assets must be returned in full, without delay.
   •These rules are shaped by past failures like FTX, where mismanagement led to consumer losses.

   3. 𝐃𝐢𝐬𝐜𝐥𝐨𝐬𝐮𝐫𝐞𝐬 𝐚𝐧𝐝 𝐨𝐩𝐞𝐫𝐚𝐭𝐢𝐨𝐧𝐚𝐥 𝐬𝐚𝐟𝐞𝐠𝐮𝐚𝐫𝐝𝐬 𝐚𝐫𝐞 𝐜𝐞𝐧𝐭𝐫𝐚𝐥
   •Issuers must publish stablecoin supply and backing asset details at least quarterly.
   •Redemption terms and asset composition must be kept clear and current.
   •Daily reconciliations are required to address shortfalls or excess supply promptly.

   4. 𝐓𝐡𝐞 𝐔𝐊 𝐢𝐬 𝐛𝐮𝐢𝐥𝐝𝐢𝐧𝐠 𝐟𝐨𝐫 𝐠𝐥𝐨𝐛𝐚𝐥 𝐜𝐨𝐦𝐩𝐚𝐭𝐢𝐛𝐢𝐥𝐢𝐭𝐲
   •The framework aligns with international standards from the FSB, IOSCO, and FATF.
   •This reduces compliance friction for global firms while upholding consumer protections.

   5. 𝐌𝐮𝐥𝐭𝐢-𝐜𝐮𝐫𝐫𝐞𝐧𝐜𝐲 𝐦𝐨𝐝𝐞𝐥𝐬 𝐚𝐧𝐝 𝐬𝐲𝐬𝐭𝐞𝐦𝐢𝐜 𝐩𝐚𝐲𝐦𝐞𝐧𝐭 𝐮𝐬𝐞𝐬 𝐚𝐫𝐞 𝐢𝐧 𝐬𝐜𝐨𝐩𝐞
   •The initial focus is on single-fiat stablecoins, but the FCA is exploring how to regulate multi-currency designs.
   •It is also coordinating with the Bank of England on stablecoins used in systemic payment systems.
   •Future consultations will cover governance, market abuse, and consumer duty.

With over 40 percent of European crypto activity involving stablecoins and 12 percent of UK adults now owning crypto, the stakes are high.

4*.*[The US GENIUS Act is not just about regulating stablecoins — it’s a referendum on who gets to control the future of money.](https://www.pymnts.com/politics/2025/proposed-genius-act-amendments-could-reshape-traditional-financial-services/) The GENIUS Act now progressing through the US Senate marks a pivotal moment in redefining the architecture of money itself. At first glance, this is just a bill about crypto. In reality, it’s a geopolitical inflection point. If passed, the Act would legitimise private, dollar-pegged digital currencies backed by U. Treasuries turning stablecoins into tools of monetary policy, financial infrastructure, and perhaps even global influence. For Washington, they could become a stealth buyer of U. debt, helping to suppress interest rates and extend the life of the US$ dominance without overt central bank intervention. But regulation is a double-edged sword. The bill now carries over 120 amendments some unrelated to crypto including credit card rate caps and trade powers. This exposes a deeper anxiety: the fear that once programmable, decentralised money is normalised, control will slip away from governments and into the hands of tech platforms, banks, or worse code itself. As the GENIUS Act nears a vote, we are watching more than a policy debate. We are witnessing the opening moves of a larger contest over **who gets to design the rules of money in the 21st century**: elected governments, private corporations, or distributed networks? This isn’t just financial reform. It’s the beginning of a new monetary operating system one that could either decentralise opportunity or centralise power under new digital monopolies. The stakes are nothing less than sovereignty in the programmable age.

### 5. [Walmart and Amazon are actively exploring the launch of their own US dollar-backed stablecoins,](https://x.com/APompliano/status/1933497889013076228) When the world’s largest retailers consider issuing their own digital dollars, they aren’t just innovating payments—they’re challenging the role of traditional banks and even central banks. A Walmart Coin or Amazon Dollar could allow them to:

* Create closed-loop economies for suppliers, shoppers, and partners
* Offer instant refunds, loyalty rewards, and credit natively on-chain
* Internalize payment infrastructure—reducing reliance on Visa, Mastercard, or SWIFT

Stablecoins let money be programmable and payments automated. Imagine:

* Discounts or coupons that expire in real time
* Conditional payments to suppliers based on inventory turnover
* Microcredit lines issued to shoppers at checkout

This isn’t just about efficiency. It’s about programmable consumer behaviour embedded directly into financial flows.

If these stablecoins are pegged to the U.S. dollar (as is likely), Amazon and Walmart could export a private version of the dollar globally. For customers in emerging markets, this may become a more trusted and usable medium of exchange than local fiat or even traditional bank accounts.

This mirrors what Meta’s failed **Libra/Diem** project tried to do—only now with companies that have built-in distribution to billions.

Governments are unlikely to welcome this unchecked. The launch of megacorp-issued stablecoins would:

* Trigger antitrust scrutiny over monetary power
* Force the hand of central banks to accelerate their CBDC efforts
* Raise urgent questions around privacy, AML/KYC, and consumer protection

The question is no longer if money will become programmable and digital—it’s who will control it:

* **States?** Through central bank digital currencies (CBDCs)
* **Corporations?** Through brand-based stablecoins
* **Networks?** Through decentralized alternatives like USDC or DAI

If Amazon and Walmart enter this arena, they won’t just be competing with FinTechs. They’ll be building financial operating systems with the scale to rival nations and faccelerate mainstream adoption, reshape payment infrastructure, and further shift power from banks to tech-driven platforms.

Are we going back to 1860’s when there were over 8,000 issuers of US Dollars? Stablecoins from retail giants could redefine not just how we pay, but who governs money in the digital age. This would mark a shift from “money as state infrastructure” to “money as a platform feature”.

[The Digital Wallet Revolution: How It's Reshaping Consumer Behaviour and Payment Infrastructure](https://www.finextra.com/blogposting/28662/the-digital-wallet-revolution-how-its-reshaping-consumer-behaviour-and-payment-infrastructure)- The leather wallet is dead. In its place: the digital wallet, not as a payment gimmick but as the operating system of modern finance. Digital wallets are reshaping far more than checkout flows—they’re redefining identity, trust, and financial inclusion. With seamless UX, biometric security, and real-time data feedback, they’re turning transactions into relationships and platforms into ecosystems. For merchants, they reduce friction and cart abandonment; for consumers, they blend spending, saving, and identity into a unified, mobile-first interface. But this is more than a fintech upgrade—it’s a rewiring of financial infrastructure. In emerging markets, wallets are leapfrogging banks. In developed economies, they’re replacing physical cards, loyalty schemes, even travel documents. Wallets are no longer the last mile of payments—they are the first point of financial presence. Yet challenges loom: fragmented standards, regulatory ambiguity, and a digital divide could undermine momentum.

Digital wallets aren’t just speeding up payments—they’re changing who gets included, who gets excluded, and who gets to shape the next generation of economic interaction.

The future isn’t just cashless—it’s wallet-first.
