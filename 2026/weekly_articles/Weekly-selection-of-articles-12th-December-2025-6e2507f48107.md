1. [**IMF: Understanding Stablecoins**](https://www.imf.org/-/media/files/publications/dp/2025/english/usea.pdf). Stablecoins are moving from crypto plumbing to the edges of the mainstream financial system. For banks, that shift is both an opportunity and a warning. Their rapid growth, with the largest US Dollar-backed coins now over $300billion in circulation, reflects persistent weaknesses in cross-border payments: high costs, long settlement chains and limited transparency. Stablecoins expose these inefficiencies with unsettling clarity. For global banks, the immediate implication is competitive pressure. Stablecoins promise near-instant settlement and lower fees, particularly for remittances and corporate treasury flows. That challenges correspondent banking revenues and forces banks to rethink how they provide cross-border liquidity, FX and cash management. At the same time, the reserve-backed structure of stablecoins ties them tightly to the traditional system. Banks remain custodians of reserves, distributors, liquidity providers and compliance gatekeepers. In practice, stablecoins may disintermediate some processes while deepening reliance on bank balance sheets and risk controls. The risks are equally material. Widespread stablecoin use could accelerate dollarisation in vulnerable economies and complicate capital controls, heightening systemic and regulatory scrutiny. Fragmented global rules also raise operational complexity for banks operating across jurisdictions. The strategic choice is clear. Banks can treat stablecoins as a peripheral threat, or integrate them as a new settlement rail, embedded within regulated balance sheet, compliance and liquidity frameworks. The winners are likely to be those that do both: modernise legacy payment infrastructure while selectively adopting tokenised money, rather than ceding the terrain to non-bank issuers.

[**Banning stablecoin remuneration will not protect banks’ deposits**](https://www.omfif.org/2025/12/banning-stablecoin-remuneration-will-not-protect-banks-deposits/?mc_cid=e8bf28bc54&mc_eid=8fd65fb32f)Regulators are grappling with how to accommodate stablecoins within the financial system without destabilising traditional banking. OMFIF argues that prohibiting interest or rewards on stablecoin holdings, a common feature of recent regulatory regimes, is a **blunt policy tool** that does little to bolster financial stability and may inadvertently introduce new cyclical risks. Rather than immune, both stablecoins and bank deposits respond to interest-rate expectations; stripping stablecoins of remuneration merely shifts demand in unpredictable ways, potentially exacerbating liquidity volatility and flight-to-safety flows. For **commercial banks**, the analysis has three key implications:
8. **Deposit competition**: Stablecoins with attractive yield structures could siphon deposits, a core funding base for banks, particularly if regulatory frameworks allow issuers to remunerate holders or earn interest on central bank reserves.
9. **Funding stability**: Banks may face higher **funding costs and reduced core deposits**, pressuring net interest margins, credit extension, and liquidity buffers, especially during stress periods when stablecoin redemptions accelerate.
10. **Regulatory arbitrage**: Divergent rules (e.g., EU’s MiCA vs US frameworks) could see capital and deposits shift toward jurisdictions or stablecoin vehicles with more favourable yield and regulatory treatment, challenging banks’ domestic franchises.

At an **economic level,** simplistic remuneration bans do not eliminate the structural forces driving stablecoin uptake. If stablecoins continue to evolve and regulators remain reactive, banks could see **deposit fragmentation, tighter lending capacity,** and **greater competitive pressure** in payments and savings, forcing them to innovate, integrate tokenised instruments, or risk margin erosion. Stablecoin regulation must therefore balance safety with market functionality to avoid unintended distortions in credit, liquidity and monetary policy transmission.
