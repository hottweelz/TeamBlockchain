This is a glimpse into a future where money moves as seamlessly as data, transforming global finance infrastructure at its core. If scaled, projects like Meridian FX could render legacy payment rails obsolete, challenge SWIFT’s dominance, and accelerate the adoption of tokenised central bank money or stablecoins in cross-border trade. BUT while it is no longer *if* settlement can be instantaneous and risk-free, it is whether the global financial system is ready to embrace this level of transparency, efficiency, and disintermediation. This is forcing institutions to rethink their role in a world where technology, not trust, guarantees value exchange.

The rise of stablecoins presents a double-edged sword for banks. On one hand, a significant migration of value into stablecoins could erode traditional bank deposits, undermining the core funding base banks rely on to provide affordable lending to the economy. Fewer deposits could translate into higher funding costs and greater pressure on credit availability. However, banks are uniquely positioned to leverage their expertise and trust to offer critical services around the stablecoin ecosystem—ranging from providing seamless fiat on- and off-ramps, to managing stablecoin cash reserves, safeguarding custody assets, and facilitating liquidity operations like Treasury purchases and repo transactions for stablecoin issuers. Some banks are already moving from defence to offense. MUFG’s startup Progmat is pioneering the use of stablecoin rails to replace SWIFT for cross-border payments, allowing businesses to issue instructions through familiar banking interfaces while gaining the benefits of instant settlement. Others, like BNY Mellon, are positioning themselves as primary banking partners to major issuers like Circle, while Standard Chartered is arguably the most aggressive among traditional players, forging partnerships with issuers such as Paxos and StraitsX, participating in stablecoin ventures in Hong Kong, and supporting cross-border foreign exchange settlements through its subsidiary Zodia Markets using stablecoins. Ultimately, the success of stablecoins will hinge not just on technological promise but on real-world delivery—proving that they can offer greater efficiency, broader accessibility, and true monetary stability while navigating the evolving regulatory frameworks emerging across global financial systems. Banks that understand this inflection point not as a threat but as an opportunity for reinvention may find themselves not sidelined by the future of money, but instrumental in shaping it.

[Stablecoin Legislation: Innovation or Trojan Horse for Big Tech Finance?](https://time.com/7274507/stablecoin-legislation-genius-act-musk/) **-** The STABLE Act—the U.S. bill advancing stablecoin regulation thus is it increasing likely Congress will enshrine stablecoins into law this year. On the surface, the bipartisan push seems like a win: stablecoins, pegged to the dollar, would bolster U.S. financial dominance, speed up transactions globally, and make payments cheaper and more secure. However, critics warn that Big Tech could be the real winner. Under current drafts of the STABLE and GENIUS Acts, non-financial companies would be free to issue stablecoins, provided they get regulatory approval. That opens the door for firms like **Amazon**, **Meta**, and **X** (formerly Twitter) to mint their own digital currencies—embedding themselves deeper into consumers’ financial lives. Imagine a world where Amazon stablecoins are used at Whole Foods, on Prime, at the Washington Post. Not only would Big Tech gain an unprecedented financial footprint, but billions of dollars that would traditionally support loans and credit markets would simply sit as reserves—sapping economic productivity. History offers cautionary tales. In China, Tencent and Alibaba’s unchecked dominance over payments forced a government crackdown, tightening state control. Could U.S. regulators later regret giving Big Tech this kind of leverage? As the U.S. edges closer to stablecoin legislation, the stakes are enormous. Stablecoins could revolutionise finance or they could privatise it. Once Big Tech captures banking, closing the door again may be impossible. The real question isn’t whether stablecoins are coming—but whether the financial system remains public and competitive, or quietly becomes a corporate fiefdom.

This positions Mastercard as a key orchestrator between TradFi and crypto, bridging the current UX gap by enabling wallets, settlement tools, on-chain remittances, and programmable money flows through a familiar network. Yet, adoption remains fragile. Regulatory uncertainty, merchant hesitation, and lack of consumer urgency still threaten stablecoins’ breakout moment. Mastercard’s bet? Make stablecoins boringly useful — as seamless as a tap-to-pay transaction. The message is clear: **if stablecoins are to scale, they must disappear into the background of finance — and Mastercard plans to be the one to make that happen.**
