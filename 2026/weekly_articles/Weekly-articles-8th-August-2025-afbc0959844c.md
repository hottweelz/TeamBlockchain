**Weekly articles 8th August 2025**

[**1. CME Group’s exploration of tokenization highlights how traditional financial infrastructure is undergoing a foundational redesign -**](https://www.ledgerinsights.com/cme-expands-on-tokenization-plans/)Rather than embracing public stablecoins and decentralised protocols, CME is aligning with Google Cloud’s permissioned ledger to tokenise commercial bank money and collateral, aiming for 24/7 asset mobility within a tightly regulated ecosystem. This signals a deeper truth: the institutions that built the legacy financial system are now selectively adopting blockchain not to disrupt themselves, but to fortify their dominance with faster, programmable rails. CME’s explosive crypto derivatives growth, $12 billion in daily notional trading and rising institutional interest, shows the appetite for exposure is real. But that activity remains largely financialised, decoupled from the more foundational questions of how value is issued, settled, and stored.

In short, CME isn’t betting on the decentralization of finance, it’s betting on its modernisation. The most profound use case for blockchain may not be in disrupting Wall Street, but in letting Wall Street own the rails.

**2.** [**Ex Revolut staff raise €6.3million to build out an API powered FX service**](https://www.finextra.com/newsarticle/46386/revolut-alumnus-extends-seed-round-to-63-million-to-grow-payments-network)**-** Due, a FinTech company created by Ex Revolut staff is quietly dismantling the outdated machinery of cross-border finance not by launching a new currency, but by abstracting the complexity behind a single API. In an age where data streams instantly across borders, moving money still feels clunky, slow, and expensive. Due flips that script by combining local payment rails, real-time FX, and blockchain settlement into one unified layer, turning the global economy into a single, liquid network. What’s radical isn’t the technology it’s the invisibility of it. By embedding its infrastructure into other FinTech platforms, Due becomes a silent enabler of seamless, borderless finance. Stablecoins aren’t the product; they’re the protocol layer. And APIs, not apps, are the new frontier of disruption.

As global liquidity fractures along geopolitical lines, the ability to route around traditional financial chokepoints becomes not just a convenience, but a strategic advantage. In this world, the winners won’t be those who issue money, but those who move it fastest, cheapest, and everywhere. Due is betting that programmable money, embedded in everything, will be the real engine of financial transformation.

**3.** [**UK crypto investors hail regulatory changes as ‘pivotal moment’-**](https://www.ft.com/content/25de4af3-135e-4370-9fd3-2ae0406f6be8?accessToken=zwAGO8Rsd2JAkc8l3krzE15DcNOf0yrgQG9r6A.MEYCIQDS2UekN8r8fLiTpdw30iS6I-PdU7wTdL1eif0XzjSEvgIhAJjmPbRUylNd3DNRpqy-GIGZcN1Ylg7HzW4VF-V8IOsU&sharetype=gift&token=c4946f07-de3b-4e49-97cb-891eb9826d10)  The UK’s decision to allow retail investors access to crypto exchange traded notes (ETNs) is being framed as a generational shift in financial policy a watershed moment comparable to the “Big Bang” reforms of the 1980s. By lifting the retail ban, the Financial Conduct Authority isn’t just updating a rule it’s validating an entire asset class that has long sat at the margins of mainstream finance. For years, UK regulators positioned crypto as too volatile, too opaque, and too risky for retail. But the FCA’s recognition that the market has matured combined with growing institutional involvement and improved transparency signals a recalibration of risk in the age of digital assets. This isn’t just about providing access to Bitcoin and Ethereum via regulated markets; it’s about acknowledging that the boundaries between traditional finance and crypto are dissolving. The FCA is no longer simply protecting retail investors from harm it’s enabling them a regulated on-ramp to an asset class that has become deeply embedded in global capital markets. In doing so, it’s catching up to the US and Europe, where digital asset ETFs and ETNs have already amassed billions in AUM. What was once dismissed as speculative frenzy is now being quietly integrated into portfolios, platforms, and policy frameworks.

The move legitimises not just the assets, but also the infrastructure: issuers like Fidelity, 21Shares, and WisdomTree can now build in London what they’ve already built in Zurich or New York. Yet the UK’s progress is still partial limited to Bitcoin and Ether and excluding leveraged products or broader crypto exposure. The FCA’s cautious optimism may slow innovation, but it also sets the stage for methodical expansion. In that sense, this is not a pivot to crypto anarchy, but a deliberate step toward making digital assets foundational to the modern financial system. The message is clear: permissionless assets are being folded into permissioned finance, and retail investors are now part of that evolution.

[**4. China tests out stablecoins amid fears of capital outflows -**](https://archive.ph/Xt2IM#selection-1583.0-1583.58) China's cautious embrace of stablecoins reveals a deeper paradox at the heart of its financial ambitions: it wants to harness a decentralised innovation to achieve a highly centralised goal globalising the renminbi. As dollar-backed stablecoins grow into an informal layer of U.S. monetary influence, Chinese policymakers are beginning to see stablecoins as tools of economic diplomacy and power projection. Yet the very features that make stablecoins so effective borderless movement, programmability, and independence from traditional banking also threaten China’s rigid control over capital flows and currency convertibility. The experimental solution is Hong Kong, a regulatory sandbox where stablecoins can be tested without fully loosening Beijing’s grip. The city’s new stablecoin licensing regime permits fiat-backed tokens but under strict conditions: limited issuance, B2B use cases only, and intense scrutiny over reserves and compliance. Behind this lies a clear message China may be willing to weaponise blockchain for monetary advantage, but not at the cost of domestic stability.

State-owned enterprises and major banks are already quietly lining up to participate, indicating strong institutional interest in payment innovation. But the rollout remains deliberately restrained, with only one major bank expected to receive a license in the initial phase. Meanwhile, the global stablecoin market, dominated by US Dollar-pegged stablecoins, continues to pull further ahead enhancing U.S. financial hegemony through programmable liquidity that China cannot yet match. The question facing Beijing is whether it can reconcile the decentralising logic of blockchain with its own command-and-control economic model. If stablecoins are indeed the next generation of cross-border finance, China risks being left behind unless it finds a way to scale its efforts without undermining its internal safeguards. What unfolds in Hong Kong will offer a glimpse of whether stablecoins can serve as instruments of Chinese statecraft or whether their very nature resists that purpose. For now, China’s stablecoin future remains a balancing act between technological ambition and geopolitical caution.

[**5. Stablecoins can support financial safety**](https://www.ft.com/content/3c06170c-0d03-4838-a5b8-f58a07715f20?accessToken=zwAGO9qjkxKQkc88BhcMDQNIONOluPWKB3FfIA.MEQCIDzppKV5CE6_IbNzrzVlkcmQObfe6vets_Q9lh7GHT_eAiBBLuiAZ3ydIOEaSdtWJfy5_gJAAgHZe4RdLeXN2L-PMA&sharetype=gift&token=3efbc2f1-a40a-4fe9-a398-324b127fb8b8). Fears that they make it easier for criminals to move money around are based on a misconception - The promise of stablecoins is not simply that they are faster or cheaper than legacy systems, but that they represent a fundamental reimagining of how money can move in a globalised, digital economy. In a financial world still built on creaking, batch-based infrastructure, stablecoins operate at internet speed transferring value in seconds, across borders, at negligible cost. This is not theoretical; it is already happening, and in ways that are forcing policymakers to reconsider what “settlement” even means. Critics often frame stablecoins as a boon for criminals, but that narrative collapses under scrutiny. Blockchains are public ledgers, immutable and globally accessible, making them far better for tracing illicit flows than the opaque, jurisdiction-bound corridors of traditional banking. Cases from the US justice department, including multi-million dollar seizures tied to stablecoin use, have shown how their transparency can turn a suspected liability into an investigative asset. Compared to cash which is untraceable and unfreezable stablecoins offer an unprecedented blend of auditability and responsiveness.

The real risk lies not in their criminal utility, but in their design. Without rigorous standards on reserves, audits, and operational resilience, some stablecoins have already undermined trust. That is why the emergence of regulatory frameworks like America’s bipartisan GENIUS Act and Europe’s MiCA is so pivotal. They codify a model where issuers must fully back tokens with highly liquid assets often US Dollars or Treasuries turning the stablecoin market into both a payments revolution and, indirectly, a buyer base for sovereign debt. In this light, the debate is no longer about whether stablecoins will matter, but about who will define their architecture. The outcome will determine whether this technology becomes a cornerstone of a more efficient, dollar-led global financial order or a fragmented, contested layer of competing monetary ecosystems. Either way, the age of slow money is ending, and the infrastructure of the next era is being built in real time.

[**6. AI’s Dual Impact on Banking’s Future**](https://www.linkedin.com/pulse/ai-banking-fundamentals-how-artificial-intelligence-reshaping-armani-0vfpe/)**-** AI is becoming the invisible engine redefining the sector’s competitive edge. The pace is staggering: a market on track to exceed $64 billion by 2030, growing at more than 30% annually, is not just reshaping workflows but reimagining the very architecture of financial services. Where legacy banking once relied on manual review, siloed data, and incremental upgrades, AI delivers real-time intelligence at scale detecting fraud in milliseconds, interpreting complex legal agreements in seconds, and tailoring services down to the individual customer’s behavioural fingerprint. Early adopters are already reporting 20% gains in operational efficiency and measurable improvements in client satisfaction, proof that AI’s payoff is both technological and strategic. It’s enabling a fundamental shift from reactive problem-solving to proactive opportunity creation anticipating risks before they materialise, identifying investment prospects before markets react, and extending sophisticated financial services to populations that traditional models could never reach profitably.

Hyper-personalisation raises privacy stakes, predictive credit scoring tests fairness, and automated compliance challenges regulators to keep pace. In this context, the most forward-looking banks are not only investing in machine learning models but embedding ethical AI frameworks into their core operations, understanding that in finance, credibility is as valuable as capital. The real question is no longer whether AI will transform banking, but how quickly the laggards will find themselves obsolete. In a sector where speed, trust, and intelligence define the winners, AI isn’t just a tool it’s the new balance sheet.
