1. **[The impact as AI, Web3.0 and robotics converge](https://followin.io/en/feed/21527109)** Robotics is undergoing its most consequential shift since the industrial robot arm: the convergence of embodied AI, autonomous robotics, and blockchain-based coordination. What began as vertically integrated hardware, servos, sensors, controllers,has evolved into a layered ecosystem where intelligence, not mechanics, defines value creation. Industrial robots remain the only fully mature segment, but humanoid robots are emerging as the next "universal labour interface," capable of operating in human-centric environments without redesigning infrastructure. Yet commercial viability remains distant, constrained by energy limits, fragile perception pipelines, high costs, and a scarcity of real-world training data. Embodied AI changes the paradigm. Unlike generative AI, which reasons in language, embodied systems learn through physical interaction, imitation learning, reinforcement learning, and simulation-to-reality pipelines. The future advances from hardware to data to generalisable world-models capable of predictive action. The bottleneck is no longer mechanics, but scalable data collection. Web3.0 enters not as a speculative overlay but as institutional infrastructure: machine identities, verifiable task execution, on-chain settlement, and decentralised governance for autonomous robot fleets. Over time, robots could operate as economic agents, earning, paying, and upgrading themselves. The question is no longer whether machines will work, but how they will be accounted for, owned, and governed.

[**Elon Musk on the future of jobs and AI, 'My prediction is that work will be optional'**](https://www.techradar.com/ai-platforms-assistants/elon-musk-on-the-future-of-jobs-and-ai-my-prediction-is-that-work-will-be-optional) AI is rewriting the economic contract: currencies long backed implicitly by human labour are drifting toward stores of asset-based value as automation hollows out wage income. With robotaxis undercutting drivers and humanoid labour costing under £1 an hour, governments face a fiscal reckoning as income-tax bases erode. As labour loses primacy, banks, wallets and even money itself undergo a structural shift where AI, automation and asset ownership, not work, determine value, revenue and financial power. Elon Musk’s latest provocation that “*work will be optional*” within 10–20 years and that “*currency becomes irrelevant*” lands at a moment when AI’s advance is already reshaping labour markets at historic speed. McKinsey estimates up to 92 million US jobs may be displaced by the end of the decade; Goldman Sachs warns of 300 million globally. If labour becomes optional, taxation, the anchor of fiscal sovereignty, becomes unstable. In the UK, over half of government revenue derives from income-based taxes; in the US, the figure is above 80%. A post-labour economy therefore implies not liberation but a profound restructuring of fiscal architecture, monetary policy and welfare provision. Musk’s optimism that AI and robotics will “eliminate poverty” offers no mechanism for redistribution in a world where capital, not labour, captures value. Musk may be right that automation will redefine work, but without a blueprint for societal adaptation, the risk is less utopia than economic dislocation.

The ECB highlights a structural fragility: corporate-issued stablecoins rely on investors’ belief in one-to-one redemption. [**Could the UK steal a march internationally as Bank of England warms to stablecoins?**](https://www.finextra.com/blogposting/30220/unpacking-the-bank-of-englands-proposed-regime-for-stablecoins)The GENIUS Act has catalysed a surge in U.S. dollar stablecoins, now a $308 billion market and 99% of global supply and global regulators are racing to respond. In the UK, momentum is finally building. The Bank of England has published its most detailed proposals yet for a regime governing sterling-denominated systemic stablecoins, signalling a shift from scepticism to structured engagement. The Bank’s consultation, designed to complement the FCA’s crypto roadmap, lays the groundwork for stablecoins to function at mass scale within the payments system. But it also sketches a notably conservative framework. Any sterling stablecoin deemed systemic must be issued by a UK-incorporated entity, hold its backing assets domestically, and operate under joint Bank/FCA supervision. Banks themselves are barred from issuing such coins unless they create ring-fenced non-deposit subsidiaries, an opening for fintech challengers. The Bank also reserves the right to designate exchanges, wallets and payment processors as systemic, extending oversight far beyond issuers. In a significant departure from U.S. and EU approaches, systemic issuers could receive accounts at the central bank, with 40% of reserves held in Bank of England money—effectively rendering such tokens quasi-CBDCs.

[**Why Custody, Not Coins, Will Shape the Next Financial System**](https://fintechmagazine.com/news/digital-asset-infrastructure-cryptos-hidden-foundation)The digital asset industry has always been noisy at the surface, yet the quiet work happening underneath will determine whether digital assets mature beyond speculative cycles. Pricing frenzies obscure a more consequential reality: **custody, compliance and settlement infrastructure—not tokens—will decide which assets survive institutional scrutiny.** The contrast with traditional finance remains stark. Ethereum’s 15-transactions-per-second base capacity sits dwarfed by Visa’s 65,000-per-second peak throughput. Layer-2 solutions promise relief but fragment liquidity, complicating risk management for banks needing predictable, auditable settlement. These frictions explain why venture capital continues to favour infrastructure providers such as Fireblocks, Chainalysis and Coinbase Custody, firms with recurring revenue, regulatory licensing and operational resilience. Institutional adoption introduces constraints retail markets never had to confront. Banks building custody or payment rails must integrate blockchain systems into decades-old infrastructure while meeting capital, insurance and regulatory expectations. Meanwhile, global regulatory fragmentation, MiCA in Europe, the GENIUS Act in the US, and divergent Asian regimes, forces infrastructure firms to build compliance engines capable of adapting to incompatible rulebooks. The greatest unresolved challenge remains interoperability. The collapse of cross-chain bridges, from Wormhole to Horizon, has exposed brittle architecture underpinning multi-chain operations. Institutions cannot operate on infrastructure that multiplies operational risk across each new chain. Over the next two years, the balance of power in digital assets will shift toward firms solving these unglamorous problems. Scalability, regulated custody and secure interoperability, not token launches, will determine whether blockchain technology embeds itself into global finance or remains a speculative sideshow.
