[**The global bond glut: a doom loop of financial repression -**](https://www.omfif.org/2025/08/the-global-bond-glut-a-doom-loop-of-financial-repression/)In 2005, Ben Bernanke warned of a global savings glut, where surplus capital from exporting nations drove down real interest rates and inflated the bubbles that burst in 2008. Two decades later, the world faces the mirror image: a global bond glut. Excessive issuance of sovereign debt now surpassing $100trillion has saturated markets, pushing long-term rates higher and reshaping the foundations of monetary policy.

Unlike the past, when banks absorbed this supply, today’s reliance on more fragile, procyclical non-bank financial institutions raises systemic risk. Governments, cornered by rising costs, may once again lean on financial repression, risking sovereign-bank doom loops that pit inflation control against financial stability. The question is whether central banks can navigate this new imbalance without triggering the next global crisis.

[**FED Vice Chair for supervision urges colleagues to embrace tokenisation**](https://www.federalreserve.gov/newsevents/speech/files/bowman20250819a.pdf) **–** This article demonstrates how important even the FED believe tokenisation will become When Federal Reserve Vice Chair for Supervision Michelle Bowman addressed a Wyoming crypto conference last week, she issued a challenge that regulators rarely voice: **adapt or risk irrelevance**. Bowman argued that digital assets, blockchain, and AI are not fringe experiments but foundational shifts in finance. To regulate them effectively, the Fed cannot simply observe from a distance. Her most provocative suggestion, Fed staff be allowed to hold small amounts of crypto wasn’t about speculation. It was about immersion. Just as **no one would trust a ski instructor who’s never touched the slopes,** Bowman insists regulators must learn by doing. This marks a striking cultural shift. For years, regulators treated digital assets as problems to contain. Now, Bowman is calling for a framework that is pragmatic, clear, and tech-neutral—acknowledging that programmable money, tokenized assets, and stablecoins are becoming mainstream. The GENIUS Act, which set out rules for stablecoins, is only the beginning. Skeptics will balk at regulators "dabbling" in crypto. Yet the alternative a supervisory class divorced from the systems they oversee risks even greater harm. Bowman’s wager is bold: that engagement, not abstinence, is the surest path to financial stability.

[**Should banks be telcos (and vice versa)**](https://thefinanser.com/2025/08/should-banks-be-telcos-and-vice-versa/?utm_source=rss&utm_medium=rss&utm_campaign=should-banks-be-telcos-and-vice-versa) **-** For more than three decades, predictions of a banking telecoms convergence have repeatedly surfaced. Experiments such as Rabobank’s mobile services or Orange Bank in France demonstrated the concept but failed to scale. The underlying reason was structural: banks optimise around interest income measured in basis points, whereas telecoms pursue transaction volume in data and minutes. Profit logics diverged, creating cultural and operational incompatibility.

![A diagram of company logos  AI-generated content may be incorrect.](data:image/png;base64...)

The rise of eSIM technology, however, introduces a materially different dynamic. Unlike physical SIMs, eSIMs are embedded in devices and can be provisioned remotely. Challenger banks including Revolut, Monzo, Nubank and Bunq are now incorporating eSIMs into their apps. Media narratives cast this as an attack on telecom incumbents, but the strategic implications extend beyond market share battles. The integration of eSIMs offers banks direct control over device-level authentication and communication channels. This is particularly significant for tackling Authorised Push Payment (APP) fraud, which exploits phone-based social engineering and weak verification processes. By embedding eSIM functionality into their fraud detection systems, banks could identify anomalies in real time, preventing attacks before they reach customers.

Thus, the true value proposition is not incremental revenue but avoided losses. Fraud costs UK consumers alone more than £1 billion annually, and regulatory scrutiny of APP fraud is intensifying. By positioning eSIMs as a defensive infrastructure enhancing security, lowering cross-border costs, and streamlining customer authentication challenger banks could redefine the economics of convergence. The challenge is for banks to leverage telecom infrastructure to strengthen banking resilience.

1. [**The impossible choice: tariffs, sanctions and fragmentation**](https://www.omfif.org/2025/08/the-impossible-choice-tariffs-sanctions-and-fragmentation/?mc_cid=5d5a751885&mc_eid=8fd65fb32f)(written by Udaibir Das, Senior Non-Resident Adviser at the Bank of England.) - Global finance is entering an era of deliberate fragmentation. Where post-Cold War orthodoxy maximised efficiency through integration, today’s policymakers are trading allocative efficiency for strategic resilience. The result is a system where financial institutions no longer act as universal intermediaries, but as compliance-bound gatekeepers segmented by jurisdictional lines. Tariffs exemplify the inflationary cascade of this shift. A U.S. average tariff rate of 27% directly inflates insurer claims and construction costs, forcing real-time repricing and destabilising actuarial models. Meanwhile, sanctions have become existential, banks must choose between serving Russia or retaining U.S. access. OFAC’s 2024 warning against participation in Russia’s SPFS system, coupled with the EU’s outright ban, illustrates how political choices now dictate financial architecture. Operationally, firms are recalibrating. HSBC has structurally divided east and west; JPMorgan spends billions annually on compliance technology. Asset managers accept narrower diversification, while legal teams balloon at the expense of investment staff. Regulatory conflicts multiply exponentially ten major jurisdictions yield ninety distinct contradictions raising costs by 15–40% and shrinking investment horizons from decades to quarters. The systemic consequences are severe. Developing economies face exclusion from correspondent banking networks, deepening a $3.9 trillion SDG financing gap. Even the US Dollar’s dominance is corroded by its own weaponisation, sustained less by strength than by lack of alternatives.

Udaibir believes, three futures remain: continued fragmentation with declining efficiency, systemic breakdown through cascade failures, or forced reintegration driven by crisis costs. Each path underscores a stark truth financial weaponisation destabilizes the very system it seeks to defend.

[**GenZ are digitally engaged and smarter with money than many think.**](https://www.pymnts.com/gen-z/2025/gen-z-isnt-broke-theyre-smarter-with-money-than-you-think/)**-** Gen Z’s (born between the mid-1990s and the early 2010s) are not merely the first “digital native” generation; it is the first truly **digital pragmatic** one. Their lives are not defined by technology as novelty but by technology as infrastructure 425 digital touchpoints a month forming the operating system of daily life. This is not excess screen time; it is seamless integration of finance, health, work, and play into platform logic. Contrary to narratives of institutional rejection, Gen Z is reshaping institutions from within. They treat banks, employers, and even healthcare providers less as authorities and more as services to be optimised. Credit hacks, side hustles, crypto wallets, and app-based therapy are not fringe behaviours but disciplined financial and personal strategies. The common thread is flexibility: platforms are tools to engineer outcomes, whether income diversification or on-demand wellbeing. This pragmatism is already remaking markets. Financial services are shifting toward embedded banking and micro-credit models. Healthcare increasingly follows a digital-first path, from telemedicine to app-based monitoring. Work is fragmented across traditional employment and platform gigs, with resilience measured less by stability than by optionality.

The implications extend well beyond Gen Z. Their behaviours serve as a blueprint for

the mainstream future of commerce and infrastructure. The distinction between digital and physical is dissolving, and businesses that fail to design for this system-level integration risk obsolescence. Arguably understanding GenZ is not about chasing youth trends but anticipating the architecture of tomorrow’s economy.

1. [**Digital Wallet Use Jumps to 16% of Online Purchases. How much will it be when we have widespread adoption of programmable digital money?**](https://www.pymnts.com/mobile-wallets/2025/digital-wallet-use-jumps-16percent-online-purchases/) **-** The digital economy has flattened generational differences in online shopping adoption, but beneath this apparent parity lie sharp contrasts in **what people buy, how they pay, and where they shop**. PYMNTS Intelligence’s latest survey of 2,722 U.S. consumers reveals that while eCommerce penetration for retail and travel has stabilised across age groups, categories like groceries and restaurant meals expose generational divides. Gen Z and millennials are pushing the boundary of digital commerce into everyday consumption. A Gen Z consumer is 72% more likely than a baby boomer to have purchased a restaurant meal online, while older cohorts remain tethered to physical stores for essentials. Payment preferences reinforce this split. In physical retail, debit cards dominate (42% of last purchases), reflecting budgeting discipline and real-time control. Online, however, credit cards (38%) take the lead, driven by perceived security and rewards. Digital wallets, still marginal in brick-and-mortar, have doubled their penetration online, with 16% using them for their last purchase evidence of a slow but steady shift toward embedded finance. Merchant loyalty also mirrors these payment ecosystems. Amazon dominates online, seamlessly capturing card and wallet users, while Walmart retains brick-and-mortar supremacy, especially among value-conscious debit card spenders. So has indeed **eCommerce has equalised access but not behaviour**. For merchants, success lies not in assuming uniformity, but in tailoring offerings to generational nuances in category adoption, payment psychology, and brand trust.

[**The rise of programmable payments: Coding cash for an autonomous financial future**](https://www.paymentscardsandmobile.com/the-rise-of-programmable-payments-coding-cash-for-the-future/)**-** When COBOL and Fortran first defined computing, programming was the preserve of specialists. Low-code platforms later opened software creation to the masses. Programmable payments mark a similar inflection in finance embedding conditional logic into transactions so that money moves automatically when events occur. Traditional payments are rigid: payroll hits monthly, subscriptions bill on schedule. Programmable payments replace fixed cycles with event-driven automation insurance payouts triggered by verified disaster data, or supplier invoices settled instantly upon delivery confirmation. Smart contracts on blockchains and APIs linking enterprise systems enable this shift, creating faster, cheaper, and less error-prone flows. The efficiency gains are striking. Businesses reduce manual reconciliation, cut fraud risks, and accelerate settlement. Entirely new models emerge, machine-to-machine payments for connected cars or energy grids, usage-based consumption tied directly to real-time activity, and automated escrow for global trade. Crucially, these are payments not money itself distinguished from programmable currency, which hard-codes restrictions into tokens.

Barriers remain. Legacy RTGS infrastructures struggle to accommodate conditional logic, and cross-border programmability faces thorny legal and regulatory hurdles. Yet central banks are experimenting the ECB’s Pontes and Appia projects trial linking distributed ledger platforms with existing TARGET services. The stakes extend beyond efficiency. Programmable payments mirror the automation wave reshaping manufacturing and healthcare, signalling a future where financial systems evolve from static accounts into responsive, rule-driven ecosystems. The challenge will be balancing innovation with governance: coding cash in ways that advance efficiency and inclusion without undermining resilience or freedom.
