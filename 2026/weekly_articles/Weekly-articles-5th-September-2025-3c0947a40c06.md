[**Outlook for the EU economy**](https://www.omfif.org/2025/09/is-there-any-hope-for-europe/?mc_cid=9e89b9a07a&mc_eid=8fd65fb32f) **-** Europe’s economic malaise is rooted in its over-regulation, lack of dynamism, and reluctance to embrace genuine liberalisation. Once on par with the United States in per capita income, Europe now lags significantly: U.S. real incomes outpace Germany’s by 20%, France’s by 35%, and Italy’s by 40%. Productivity growth is stagnant, particularly in southern Europe, while U.S. productivity expands at twice the European pace. This economic gap is compounded by disillusionment, 75% of Europeans report lower happiness than Americans, undermining the continent’s once lofty ambitions of becoming the “most competitive knowledge economy.” Mario Draghi’s report underscores the central problem: regulation and state intervention smother innovation, entrepreneurship, and competition. Yet instead of deregulation, Europe doubles down on state-driven industrial policy, expensive green mandates, and debt-fuelled public investment. Federalism, which could encourage experimentation, is neutralised by Brussels’ drive for uniformity, producing groupthink rather than dynamism. Immigration policy compounds the malaise: while Europe imports young workers to offset ageing demographics, rigid labour markets cannot absorb them, leaving youth unemployment above 20% in major economies. Poor integration frameworks further threaten social cohesion. Europe’s trajectory is thus one of stagnation, with regulatory excess and political paralysis blocking renewal. A path forward exists deregulation, competition, pragmatic immigration but it requires cultural and political shifts Europe shows little willingness to embrace. Without radical reform, Europe risks entrenching its decline, losing competitiveness to both the U.S. and China, and forfeiting its role as a serious economic pole in the 21st century.

[**SWIFT and Ripple look to rebuild payments and messaging services on-chain.**](https://www.ccn.com/news/crypto/swifts-ripple-trial-turn-xrp-backbone-global-payments/)**-** Could SWIFT’s live trials with Ripple’s XRP mark a watershed moment for global finance, positioning the use of blockchain technology not as a rival but as an enabler of existing infrastructure. For decades, SWIFT has dominated interbank messaging, but inefficiencies are glaring, transfers can take up to five days, fees are unpredictable, and transparency is minimal. Ripple’s XRP, by contrast, offers near-instant settlement at negligible cost, making it an ideal bridge currency for cross-border liquidity. By integrating XRP via ISO 20022 standards, SWIFT could transform international transfers, replacing the costly correspondent banking model with real-time settlement. Analysts suggest that if just 1% of SWIFT’s $150 trillion annual flows moved through XRP, it would create $1.5 trillion in demand. CEO Brad Garlinghouse argues XRP could capture 14% of SWIFT’s liquidity market by 2030, underscoring the disruptive potential. Yet risks remain as adoption depends on regulatory clarity, institutional trust, and technical scalability. For XRP, integration would validate its decade-long pitch as payments infrastructure rather than speculation. For SWIFT, it would mean embracing the very technology once seen as a threat. If successful, this experiment could redefine the rails of global finance, turning XRP from an outsider into a backbone of international liquidity. The stakes are vast: success would catalyse blockchain’s mainstream integration, while failure could entrench legacy inefficiencies for another generation.

[**EU increasingly looking at tokenisation of real-world assets**](https://www.theblock.co/post/369002/european-commission-eyes-proposals-rwa-tokenization) **-** Peter Kerstens, an adviser to the European Commission, recently highlighted the Commission's focus on the Savings and Investment Union (SIU), with proposals expected in December 2025 that will integrate tokenisation elements into the financial system. Kerstens emphasises that the tokenisation of real-world assets (RWA), including equities, debt, and derivatives, represents the next major frontier in financial innovation within the EU, overshadowing calls for an immediate update to the Markets in Crypto-Assets (MiCA) framework. The SIU aims to foster a pan-European financial market by channelling savings into cross-border investment, addressing the fragmentation of Europe’s 27 capital markets, and leveraging blockchain technology as a unifying force for overcoming national barriers. Kerstens’s view reflects a broader regulatory shift towards enabling and upgrading frameworks allowing traditional financial institutions, historically hesitant about blockchain, to more fully embrace asset tokenisation. This signals a transformation, promoting greater market efficiency, transparency, and integration across the EU's capital markets. The SIU’s initiatives are designed to modernise Europe’s financial ecosystem, increase liquidity, and support strategic priorities such as green and digital transitions. Kerstens’s remarks align with ESMA Executive Director Natasha Cazenave’s perspective that tokenisation is not just a technological evolution but a potential catalyst for transformational change in financial markets. By embedding blockchain technology into the SIU framework, the European Commission aims to make Europe a leader in financial innovation and integration, creating a more efficient, competitive, and unified capital market. Although EU is now behind the USA despite having led with MiCAR last year!

[**Bank of England’s view of the multi-money verse**](https://www.bankofengland.co.uk/speech/2025/september/sarah-breeden-keynote-speech-at-the-boe-and-warwick-business-school) **–** Sarah Breeden Deputy Governor for financial stability at Bank of England believes the UK finds itself at a crossroads in reimagining the architecture of money and payments. Her vision is: “*for the UK payments landscape is one of a “multi-money” mixed ecosystem*”. The Bank of England’s launch of RT2, a state-of-the-art Real Time Gross Settlement system, marks a decisive step in keeping central bank money relevant in an increasingly tokenised and digital financial landscape. Alongside, the design of a potential digital pound and the Digital Securities Sandbox illustrate a willingness to experiment while managing risk. Yet the challenge is not simply technological, it is about designing regulatory frameworks that can accommodate private innovations like stablecoins and tokenised deposits without undermining trust. Central bankers argue that central bank money must remain the settlement anchor for systemically important markets, while acknowledging the growing role of regulated private money in both wholesale and retail payments. The UK’s Payments Vision Delivery Committee is trying to engineer interoperability across these multiple forms of money to prevent fragmentation and ‘walled gardens.’ The tension lies in balancing innovation, which promises faster, cheaper, programmable payments, against financial stability, which demands caution. With AI, blockchain technology and quantum computing reshaping finance, the UK’s “multi-money” vision offers an ambitious blueprint. But can it reconcile openness and competition with the preservation of monetary trust, or will regulatory caution dull its edge in the global race to modernise payments and let the USA continue to pull ahead?

[**Programmable payments for autonomous financial future**](https://www.paymentscardsandmobile.com/the-rise-of-programmable-payments-coding-cash-for-the-future/)**-** Programmable payments mark a fundamental shift in financial operations, moving beyond rigid, fixed schedules to automated, dynamic transactions triggered by predefined conditions. Just as low-code platforms simplified software development, programmable payments automate financial workflows, reducing reliance on manual approvals, intermediaries, and batch processing. Enabled by smart contracts and APIs, they can release funds instantly upon delivery confirmation, settle insurance claims based on verified data, or process usage-based payments in manufacturing and IoT ecosystems. The benefits are significant: reduced costs, accelerated settlement, improved transparency, and enhanced security. Blockchain-based programmable payments create immutable audit trails, lower fraud risk, and streamline cross-border transfers by bypassing traditional correspondent banking chains. Their applications span supply chains, insurance, machine-to-machine transactions, and real-time consumer services. However, adoption faces hurdles. Legacy payment infrastructures cannot easily integrate conditional logic, while regulatory uncertainties, especially around cross-border settlements and smart contract enforceability, pose challenges. Distinction is also needed between programmable money (logic embedded in currency itself) and programmable payments (automation applied to existing funds). Despite challenges, momentum is accelerating. With central banks exploring programmable infrastructures and industry pilots expanding, programmable payments are poised to redefine finance, making transactions faster, smarter, and deeply integrated into the digital economy.

[**AI and Embedded Finance Rewrite the Banking Script**](https://thefintechtimes.com/stark-truths-ai-and-embedded-finance-rewrite-the-banking-script/)**-** The rise of agentic AI is poised to transform financial services as profoundly as the invention of cheques centuries ago. Drawing inspiration from Marvel’s J.A.R.V.I.S., AI agents are set to act as personal financial assistants, independently identifying customer needs and executing tasks on their behalf. This evolution builds on embedded finance, where financial services are integrated into non-financial customer journeys. The next step, embedded AI agents, will collapse the gap between product discovery, decision-making, and purchase into a seamless, conversational flow. For banks, this shift is destabilising. Historically, they thrived on information asymmetry, leveraging knowledge advantages over customers. But with AI and tools like ChatGPT delivering near-perfect information, often bypassing traditional websites, banks risk losing visibility and direct customer engagement. As search behaviour evolves, distribution models are collapsing, forcing banks to move “outside in”, providing modular, embedded infrastructures that others leverage at the point of need. While many institutions experiment with AI for efficiency, they risk missing the deeper shift: relevance will come not from owning customer interactions but from enabling agents and ecosystems. Regulation, trust, and contextual understanding remain hurdles, yet banks must accept they are no longer centre stage, just actors in a bigger, AI-powered financial universe.
