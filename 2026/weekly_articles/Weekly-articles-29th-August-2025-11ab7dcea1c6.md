[**Blockchain in Banking: Navigating the Inflection Point Between Risk and Reward**](https://www.ainvest.com/news/blockchain-banking-navigating-inflection-point-risk-reward-2508/) **-** The U.S. financial system is entering a decisive inflection point. Federal Reserve Vice Chair Michelle Bowman’s remarks at the Wyoming Blockchain Symposium reframed blockchain not as an experiment, but as a structural force reshaping banking, payments, and asset markets. Her signal that the Fed is eliminating “reputational risk” as a supervisory criterion, combined with the GENIUS Act’s mandate for stablecoin oversight, marks a regulatory pivot: financial authorities are no longer gatekeepers of innovation, but architects of a framework designed to accelerate adoption. This shift collides with technological reality. Tokenization, stablecoins, and blockchain-based settlement are already operational—from JPMorgan’s Onyx platform to WeBank’s enterprise-grade BCOS 4.0. The competitive stakes are clear: pioneers are using blockchain to cut costs, boost liquidity, and operate 24/7, while laggards risk entrenching inefficiencies. The strategic divide is widening. Challenger banks like WeBank, Kakao, and Zenus are embedding blockchain into their core business models, leveraging it for cross-border CBDCs, open API expansion, and Banking-as-a-Service. By contrast, traditional institutions weighed down by legacy systems face rising risks of irrelevance, even as surveys suggest blockchain adoption could yield 5–10% cost savings within five years.

For investors, two avenues emerge: fintech leaders integrating blockchain at scale, and infrastructure providers from AWS to Accenture building the rails of programmable finance. The opportunity is vast, with tokenisation projected to unlock over $10 trillion by 2030. Regulators and traditional financial services companies are beginning to realise the use of blockchain technology is not incremental, it is foundational.

[**Re- Dollarisation and the creep of US monetary power**](https://www.financialsense.com/blog/21379/redollarization-and-digital-statecraft-how-stablecoins-are-rewiring-global-power) **–** Arguably the real revolution digital money is ushering in is not about cryptocurrencies or CBDCs but **stablecoins**. US Dollar-backed stablecoins are quietly re-engineering the foundations of global finance and, in the process, **reasserting U.S. monetary dominance.** We are seeing the “re-dollarization”: the rebuilding of global financial architecture around the U.S. dollar powered by blockchain technology. The **GENIUS Act** has created regulatory clarity for US Dollar denominated stablecoins, to be backed U.S. Treasuries creating huge **demand U.S. government debt,** lowering short term interest rates and reinforcing the US Dollar’s role as the reserve currency. Unlike CBDCs, which face adoption hurdles, privacy concerns, and political resistance, stablecoins are thriving precisely because they are **market-driven,** flexible, and interoperable with existing systems. here. This on shore demand presents a subtle form of **financial statecraft** forcing a wave of demand for U.S. debt, deepening fiscal resilience without overt coercion. In effect, Washington is outsourcing the reinforcement of US Dollar primacy to private-sector innovation. Meanwhile, CBDCs are faltering. Outside of China, enthusiasm has cooled, with adoption proving sluggish and fears of surveillance undermining political will. What once looked like a threat to banks is increasingly understood as a **disciplining mechanism**: central banks raised the spectre of CBDCs to nudge financial institutions toward tokenisation, not to replace them.

The geopolitics are profound. Europe fears that dollar stablecoins could siphon savings out of the euro system, while emerging markets face renewed pressure as stablecoins make dollar access easier than ever. Offshore issuers like Tether complicate the landscape, but U.S. regulatory clarity may soon determine whose digital dollars are treated as legitimate. Kaminska frames stablecoins as a “lifeboat” carrying the financial system from the hyper-leveraged, opaque post-2008 era into a more **transparent, collateralised, and fragmented order**. They represent not just efficiency gains, but a strategic realignment: one where money is programmable, markets are restructured around collateral, and U.S. dominance is quietly reinforced through code, regulation, and Treasury bonds.

1. [**Can US. Dollar stablecoins really increase demand for US Treasuries?**](https://www.omfif.org/2025/08/do-stablecoins-increase-the-net-demand-for-us-treasury-securities/?mc_cid=82d54a884d&mc_eid=8fd65fb32f) - Stablecoins are increasingly hailed as a lifeline for government debt markets, with advocates suggesting they could boost demand for Treasuries and lower borrowing costs. Yet this optimism may be misplaced. Stablecoins, now a $230bn market, back their issuance largely with short-term Treasuries, but whether they add net demand is unclear. Much like money market funds in the 1970s–80s, which reshaped financial intermediation without demonstrably lowering aggregate borrowing costs, stablecoins may simply substitute existing liquidity balances rather than expand them. Transaction volumes, now near $950bn a month, pale in comparison to the $100tn flowing through U.S. payment systems, and high velocity means fewer coins are needed to cover large activity. Liquidity preferences, opportunity costs, and interest rate regimes will further constrain growth. Equating gross with net demand risks a partial-equilibrium fallacy, ignoring crowding-out effects. Governments should therefore temper expectations: stablecoins may deepen Treasury markets, but they cannot erase debt’s structural gravity.
2. [**Stablecoins going mainstream-**](https://dgwbirch.substack.com/p/stablecoins-are-mainstream) Brendan Greely from the Financial Times:
   "*There's nothing inherently dodgy about stablecoins. But there is something inherently dodgy about banking, which is why countries build elaborate regulatory regimes to protect deposits*. Stablecoins have rapidly shifted from crypto experimentation to mainstream financial infrastructure. Stripe’s acquisitions of Bridge and Privy, with more than 75 million wallets, signal their integration into global payments. Unlike their original algorithmic definition, today’s stablecoins are fiat-backed digital tokens, typically pegged to the U.S. dollar through reserves in Treasuries or bank deposits. Together, Tether ($149B) and Circle’s USDC ($62B) represent nearly $240B in circulation, drawing the close attention of U.S. policymakers. Their use is increasingly practical: SpaceX employs them to repatriate revenue from Argentina and Nigeria, while ScaleAI pays overseas contractors in stablecoins. Surveys show users in emerging markets view them as a core payment solution for remittances, commerce, and currency conversion.

Yet stablecoins’ growth is politically fraught. US Dollar-pegged stablecoins risk “digital dollarisation,” undermining monetary sovereignty in Europe and elsewhere. The Banque de France has warned of “de-Europeanisation,” fuelling urgency for a digital euro, while U.S. regulators debate whether private stablecoins should ultimately give way to a CBDC. Still, stablecoins are not banks; they lack credit creation risks but offer programmable, instant settlement rails that banks might leverage. Their rise forces a rethinking of money itself—whether central banks embrace them or risk ceding influence to private issuers.

[**Google looks to challenge SWIFT**](https://cryptorank.io/news/feed/034a6-google-cloud-builds-neutral-layer-1-blockchain-in-biggest-threat-to-swift-yet) **-** Google Cloud’s **Universal Ledger (GCUL)** represents a bold step into financial infrastructure. Aims to provide programmable money and settlement tools tailored for institutions, not retail speculation. Its biggest **strengths** are neutrality, scalability, and trust in Google’s infrastructure. Unlike Stripe’s Tempo or Circle’s Arc, which are tied to their own ecosystems, GCUL markets itself as an **open, vendor-agnostic ledger,** making it easier for banks, asset managers, and governments to adopt without competitive conflicts. Benefits **include 24/7 settlement, reduced reconciliation costs, stronger compliance workflows, and programmable financial logic**. The potential to run commercial bank money directly on-chain could streamline payments and capital markets globally.

But the **cons** are significant. Entrusting core money infrastructure to a single tech giant raises concerns about **centralisation, data sovereignty, and systemic dependency.** Regulatory clarity is still evolving, and governments may hesitate to let [Google mediate monetary systems](https://cointelegraph.com/news/google-plans-universal-ledger-blockchain-institutional-layer?utm_source=chatgpt.com). Banks may also resist outsourcing their settlement backbone, fearing a loss of autonomy. Technically, [GCUL remains in private testnet](https://cryptorank.io/news/feed/034a6-google-cloud-builds-neutral-layer-1-blockchain-in-biggest-threat-to-swift-yet?utm_source=chatgpt.com), leaving scalability, governance, and resilience unproven. Ultimately, GCUL is less an existential threat to banks than a **strategic catalyst:** forcing them to adapt to programmable finance, or risk being outpaced by tech-native infrastructure.

6.[**Zero-Trust Meets Blockchain: A New Framework For Fintech Security**](https://www.forbes.com/sites/digital-assets/2025/08/28/zero-trust-meets-blockchain-a-new-framework-for-fintech-security/)For decades, financial security meant fortifying perimeters, firewalls, passwords, intrusion systems but most breaches came from within. Zero-trust architectures reframed the problem, shifting to continuous verification of every user, device, and action. Yet in practice, zero-trust still relies on centralised policy engines vulnerable to insider abuse or compromise. This is where blockchain may mark the next leap. By embedding access controls into smart contracts, security rules become transparent, immutable, and auditable. No single administrator can wield unchecked power; every policy change is logged, every permission visible, and consensus replaces hierarchy. For FinTechs navigating open banking’s API sprawl, digital asset custody, and regulatory scrutiny, such decentralisation could redefine trust. Such a blockchain-powered platform means regulators gain immutable audit trails, firms achieve operational resilience, and customers see cryptographically enforced guarantees rather than corporate promises. But the hurdles are formidable. On-chain verification risks latency and cost, privacy may be compromised if access logs are public, and governance disputes loom when authority is dispersed. Hybrid models, keeping core policies on-chain, real-time checks off-chain seem most feasible.

Banks are already piloting tokenised identities, payment processors are testing blockchain audit trails, and digital asset firms deploy multi-party computation to distribute trust. A blockchain-zero-trust fusion feels less radical disruption than an inevitable evolution. If successful, it reframes security not as a patchwork expense but as shared infrastructure, tamper-resistant, regulator-ready, and built into the very rails of finance. In a sector where breaches can destroy overnight, such trust could prove the rarest and most valuable currency.
