The value is not peer-to-peer transfers; it is institutional settlement at internet speed. The SEC’s 2% haircut signals that, at least for regulators, stablecoins are moving from curiosity to infrastructure.
2. [**Why HMRC Doesn’t Want You to Understand how the wealthy reduce their tax: Buy, Borrow, Die**](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=tcpBWjAikxM). This short YouTube video explains how, **Buy, borrow, die**, a long-standing wealth strategy is used by high-net-worth individuals to minimise taxes legally. The idea is simple. First, **buy** appreciating assets such as shares, real estate, or private businesses and hold them long term. As these assets rise in value, no tax is due because unrealised gains are not taxed. Next, **borrow** against those assets instead of selling them. Loans are not taxable income, so the owner can fund lifestyle spending, investments, or philanthropy without triggering capital gains tax. Finally, at death, **die**: in many jurisdictions, assets receive a step-up in cost basis, wiping out accumulated capital gains for heirs. Stablecoins can make this strategy more effective by removing friction from the borrowing and spending stages. Instead of relying solely on traditional banks, asset owners can access credit lines settled in stablecoins, often faster and with fewer intermediaries. Loans can be drawn 24/7, globally, and at lower operational cost. Stablecoins also allow borrowed funds to be spent internationally without foreign exchange fees or banking delays, while remaining economically equivalent to cash. Crucially, stablecoins do not change the tax logic of buy, borrow, die. They **industrialise it**, making liquidity instant, portable, and programmable, so leverage becomes easier to manage, more flexible, and more efficient, while the underlying tax advantages remain intact.
3. **The Velocity Revolution: How Digital Money Could Redraw the GDP Map.** McKinsey’s February 2026 analysis of stablecoin transaction data reveals a striking divergence: raw volumes appear modest, yet the economic footprint is outsized. Stablecoins settle in seconds, not days, compressing float and releasing trapped liquidity that traditional rails lock up for 1–3 business days. Faster money velocity means every euro or dollar can cycle through the real economy more times per year, paying suppliers, funding payrolls, or funding investment without the drag of settlement delays. The macroeconomic implication is profound. Money that moves ten times faster effectively amplifies the money supply without printing a single note. McKinsey estimates that widespread adoption of instant, programmable payments could raise GDP growth by 0.5–1.5 percentage points annually in advanced economies through improved working-capital efficiency, reduced borrowing needs, and accelerated consumption. For SMEs, the effect is magnified: quicker receivables mean less reliance on expensive overdrafts, freeing capital for hiring or expansion. In emerging markets, the impact could be even larger, formalising cash economies and deepening financial inclusion.

Yet the promise carries a shadow. Higher velocity makes economies more sensitive to shocks; a sudden loss of confidence could trigger faster contagion. Banks face deposit erosion as corporates and households shift to yield-bearing stablecoins, compressing lending capacity and net interest margins. Central banks lose direct control over broad money creation, forcing a rethink of monetary-policy transmission. The deeper question: if digital money turns velocity into the dominant growth lever, does UK (or any jurisdiction) gain a structural premium, or does it trade one form of stability for another? In a programmable world, GDP growth may no longer be about how much money exists, but how quickly it moves, and who controls the speed.

The mechanism is straightforward yet profound: every euro that settles instantly can be spent, invested, or lent again within hours instead of days. For SMEs, this means quicker supplier payments and reduced reliance on expensive overdrafts. For corporates, it shrinks treasury buffers and unlocks capital for growth. For the broader economy, it amplifies monetary transmission, rate cuts reach households and firms faster, magnifying policy impact. Yet the promise carries a subtle warning. Faster money is more volatile money. In a downturn, the same velocity that fuels growth can accelerate contractions. Banks, already squeezed by deposit flight to stablecoins, may face thinner margins and higher liquidity risk as float income vanishes. The deeper question: if instant payments turn money into pure velocity, does Europe gain a structural growth premium, or does it trade one form of fragility for another? In a world of programmable flows, efficiency is no longer free.
